# Asset Research Agent Framework v2.1

## Business-Process, Moat, Historical Diagnostics, Forecasting and Valuation

---

## 1. Mission

Research an asset to determine:

1. **What economically drives its value**
2. **How the underlying business or economic system actually functions**
3. **Which decisions, processes and constraints convert demand into revenue, earnings and cash flow**
4. **Why those processes may strengthen, weaken or break**
5. **What expectations are already embedded in the current price**
6. **What must happen operationally for the investment to succeed or fail**
7. **Whether the expected return adequately compensates for risk**

The objective is not to produce a persuasive investment thesis. The objective is to produce a decision-useful, evidence-based range of outcomes whose financial forecasts can be traced back to real business processes.

The agent must not move directly from a market narrative to a revenue-growth assumption.

The required analytical chain is:

```text
End demand
→ customer economics
→ customer decision
→ budget allocation
→ product or architecture selection
→ operational deployment
→ delivery and revenue recognition
→ pricing and mix
→ cost structure
→ reinvestment and working capital
→ cash flow
→ per-share or per-unit value
```

Revenue growth, margin expansion and cash-flow growth must be the **outputs** of this chain, not unsupported starting assumptions.

## 2. Non-Negotiable Research Doctrine

### 2.1 Understand the system before valuing the asset

Do not begin with a valuation model.

First determine:

- What the asset represents
- How the business or economic system functions
- Who ultimately pays for the product, service, utility or scarcity
- What problem is being solved
- How customers decide whether to buy, use, renew, replace or stop using it
- What operational steps are required before demand becomes recognized revenue
- What constrains supply, demand, pricing, capacity, adoption or deployment
- Where bargaining power sits in the value chain
- What causes returns to accrue to the asset holder

A valuation model built before the business process is understood is incomplete, even if its formulas are mathematically correct.

### 2.2 Separate facts, estimates and speculation

Every material input must be classified as one of:

- **Disclosed fact**
- **Independent third-party data**
- **Derived estimate**
- **Analyst assumption**
- **Speculative option**

Never present an estimate as reported data.

For every material assumption, state:

- The evidence supporting it
- The causal mechanism
- The relevant time lag
- The range of plausible outcomes
- What evidence would disprove it
- Where it enters the model

### 2.3 Model causality, not correlations

Forecasts must explain the operating chain.

Examples:

```text
Revenue = Market demand × Addressable share × Win rate × Deployment rate × Monetization
```

```text
Revenue = Customers × Adoption rate × Usage × Price × Retention
```

```text
Commodity cash flow = Production × Realized price − Cash costs − Sustaining capital − Growth capital
```

```text
Lender earnings = Earning assets × Yield − Funding cost − Credit losses − Operating expense
```

Do not forecast revenue growth directly when it can be built from underlying business processes.

### 2.4 No naked growth assumptions

A forecast such as “revenue grows 20%” is prohibited unless the agent can answer:

1. What operational process creates the growth?
2. Who makes the economic decision that causes it?
3. Why would that party make the decision?
4. What capacity, funding, regulation or infrastructure is required?
5. Which competitor, substitute or incumbent loses volume or economics?
6. What is the delay between the decision and reported revenue?
7. What pricing, mix or unit-cost effects accompany the growth?
8. What cash investment is required before the growth is realized?
9. What would cause the forecast to fail?
10. Which measurable indicators would reveal failure early?

If these questions cannot be answered, the growth assumption must be rejected or explicitly classified as speculative.

### 2.5 Constrain the asset by its real market

Company, protocol or asset growth must remain consistent with:

- Total addressable demand
- Serviceable demand
- Customer budgets
- Customer return on investment
- Industry supply
- Market-share limits
- Physical production capacity
- Infrastructure availability
- Distribution reach
- Regulation
- Capital availability
- Adoption speed
- Replacement cycles
- Competitor capacity and responses

Always calculate the implied market size, market share, customer spend, installed base or resource requirement created by the forecast.

### 2.6 Avoid double counting

A driver may affect multiple parts of the business, but its economic contribution must only be counted once.

Examples:

- Subscription spending already included in customer growth cannot be added again as separate optionality.
- A new product may cannibalize an existing product.
- A commodity price increase may raise revenue but also increase input costs.
- Software value reflected in higher retention and margins cannot be added again as a separate sum-of-the-parts valuation without adjustment.
- Industry growth and company share gains cannot both be assumed from the same customer spending pool without reconciliation.
- Replacement demand cannot be added to total demand if the market forecast already includes replacement purchases.

### 2.7 Research adversarially

Do not model only how the thesis succeeds.

For every bullish driver, identify:

- How it could fail
- What evidence would disprove it
- Whether it cannibalizes another segment
- Whether competitors capture the value
- Whether customers receive sufficient economic benefit
- Whether growth requires uneconomic spending
- Whether operational capacity can support it
- Whether the market has already priced it in
- Whether management has incentives to overstate it

The agent must attempt to invalidate the thesis before accepting it.

### 2.8 Distinguish demand from monetizable demand

Interest, usage, activity or technical adoption does not automatically produce value for the asset holder.

The agent must distinguish:

```text
Potential demand
→ active demand
→ economically justified demand
→ budgeted demand
→ deployed demand
→ monetized demand
→ collected cash
```

Each transition must be tested separately.

### 2.9 Distinguish financial capacity from operational capacity

A customer may have the budget but lack the infrastructure, labor, permits, power, supply or integration ability to deploy the product.

Conversely, supply may exist without sufficient profitable demand.

The agent must separately evaluate:

- Financial capacity
- Physical capacity
- Implementation capacity
- Organizational readiness
- Economic justification
- Regulatory permission

### 2.10 Respect time lags

Every business process has a time lag.

Examples:

- Customer approval to purchase order
- Purchase order to delivery
- Delivery to deployment
- Deployment to utilization
- Utilization to reorder
- Capacity investment to production
- R&D to commercial product
- Credit underwriting to realized losses
- Customer acquisition to payback

Forecasts must map these lags explicitly. A leading indicator must not be treated as same-period revenue.

## 3. Mandatory Business-Process Analysis

For every operating company, the agent must produce a business-process map before building the financial model.

A simple business may require 5–8 process nodes. A complex company may require 10–25 nodes and separate maps for major segments.

### 3.1 Required process layers

The process map must cover, where relevant:

1. **End-market need creation**
2. **Customer problem and economic value**
3. **Customer decision process**
4. **Budget formation and capital allocation**
5. **Product, supplier or architecture selection**
6. **Procurement and contracting**
7. **Production or service capacity**
8. **Supply-chain and infrastructure constraints**
9. **Delivery, implementation or deployment**
10. **Utilization and customer outcome**
11. **Renewal, replacement, expansion or churn**
12. **Revenue recognition**
13. **Pricing and product mix**
14. **Cost formation**
15. **Working capital and cash collection**
16. **Reinvestment requirements**
17. **Competitive response**
18. **Regulatory and external constraints**

### 3.2 Mandatory process-node template

For every material process node, record:

| Field | Required analysis |
|---|---|
| Process node | The business activity or decision being analyzed |
| Starting condition | Current factual state |
| Decision maker | Customer, management, regulator, supplier, employee or market participant |
| Trigger | What causes the process to begin or change |
| Economic incentive | Why the decision maker would act |
| Required resources | Budget, capacity, infrastructure, labor, inventory, permits or technology |
| Main constraint | The bottleneck limiting the outcome |
| Possible outcomes | At least a downside, central and upside outcome |
| Competitive response | How rivals, substitutes or customers may react |
| Time lag | Delay before the financial effect appears |
| Financial line affected | Volume, price, mix, cost, capex, working capital, margin or cash flow |
| Leading indicator | Metric that moves before reported financial results |
| Failure condition | Evidence that the assumed process is breaking |
| Source quality | Fact, third-party data, estimate, assumption or speculation |
| Confidence | High, moderate or low |

### 3.3 Process-outcome tree

For each major process, construct an outcome tree rather than a single forecast.

Example:

```text
Customer demand rises
├── Customer economics improve
│   ├── Budget expands
│   │   ├── Company wins share → volume growth
│   │   └── Competitor wins share → market grows but company underperforms
│   └── Budget constrained by deployment capacity → orders delayed
├── Customer economics remain uncertain
│   ├── Pilot activity grows but production deployment stalls
│   └── Customers demand lower pricing before scaling
└── Customer economics deteriorate
    ├── Capacity digestion
    ├── cancellations or delayed orders
    └── substitution toward lower-cost alternatives
```

The agent must explain which branches form the bear, base and bull cases.

### 3.4 Bottleneck analysis

For each segment, calculate or identify:

```text
Maximum achievable output = minimum of all required bottleneck capacities
```

Possible bottlenecks include:

- Customer budget
- Production capacity
- Supplier capacity
- Raw materials
- Skilled labor
- Distribution
- Power
- Data-centre capacity
- Regulation
- Customer implementation ability
- Sales coverage
- Capital
- Balance-sheet capacity
- Inventory availability

The forecast cannot exceed the narrowest credible bottleneck without explaining how that bottleneck is resolved.

### 3.5 Customer economics

The agent must determine why the customer purchases the product.

Where possible, estimate:

```text
Customer ROI = Customer revenue gain + Cost savings + Risk reduction − Total ownership cost
```

Total ownership cost may include:

- Purchase price
- Financing cost
- Implementation
- Integration
- Training
- Labor
- Maintenance
- Power or fuel
- Switching costs
- Downtime
- Compliance

The analysis must answer:

- Is the product mission-critical or discretionary?
- Who controls the budget?
- What is the customer payback period?
- What alternative could the customer choose?
- What would cause the customer to delay, reduce or cancel spending?
- Does customer value grow faster or slower than the company’s price?

### 3.6 Competitive and game-theoretic response

Market growth does not guarantee company growth.

For every material growth opportunity, identify:

- Who currently serves the demand
- Which competitor would lose share
- Whether the competitor can cut price
- Whether customers can vertically integrate
- Whether substitutes improve as the market matures
- Whether the company’s success attracts new capacity
- Whether industry profits encourage overinvestment
- Whether customers gain bargaining power at scale

The forecast must include competitor reactions rather than assuming competitors remain passive.

### 3.7 Delivery and revenue-recognition mechanics

The agent must explain how an order becomes reported revenue.

Depending on the business, this may require:

```text
Lead generation
→ contract
→ customer financing
→ production
→ delivery
→ installation
→ customer acceptance
→ revenue recognition
→ billing
→ cash collection
```

The research must identify:

- Backlog quality
- Cancellation rights
- Order lead times
- Delivery constraints
- Acceptance conditions
- Channel inventory
- Return rights
- Revenue-recognition timing
- Receivable collection

Bookings, backlog, shipments, deployments, revenue and cash are not interchangeable.

### 3.8 Replacement, renewal and repeat-purchase mechanics

For recurring or replacement-driven businesses, model:

```text
Eligible installed base × Renewal or replacement rate × Spend per renewal
```

The agent must determine:

- Useful product life
- Contract duration
- Renewal timing
- Churn drivers
- Upgrade economics
- Replacement triggers
- Cannibalization from new products
- Customer tendency to extend asset life

### 3.9 Margin-formation process

Margins must be derived from business mechanics.

```text
Gross margin = Price − Input cost − Production cost − Distribution cost − Warranty or service cost
```

Margin outcomes should be tied to:

- Scarcity or excess supply
- Customer bargaining power
- Competitive pricing
- Product mix
- Scale economics
- Supplier pricing
- Yield or utilization
- Freight and logistics
- Channel incentives
- Warranty or credit losses
- Software or service attachment
- Inventory charges

Do not assume margin expansion merely because revenue grows.

### 3.10 Cash-conversion process

Accounting earnings are not sufficient.

Model:

```text
Cash flow = Operating profit after tax
            + Non-cash charges
            − Working-capital investment
            − Capital expenditure
            − Capitalized development
            − Customer financing
            − Other economic reinvestment
```

The agent must test whether growth requires:

- Inventory accumulation
- Supplier prepayments
- Receivables growth
- Customer financing
- Capacity reservations
- Leases
- Guarantees
- Strategic investments
- Acquisitions
- Capitalized R&D

## 4. Mandatory 20-Quarter Financial and KPI Analysis

A company forecast must be grounded in a sufficiently long operating record. For publicly listed operating companies, the default historical dataset is the **latest 20 completed fiscal quarters**, representing approximately five years.

The 20-quarter requirement is not satisfied by presenting a table. The agent must validate the data, calculate consistent metrics, diagnose changes and connect those changes to the business-process map and moat analysis.

### 4.1 Historical-data requirement

Before forecasting, collect the latest 20 completed quarters of, where applicable:

- Income statement
- Balance sheet
- Cash-flow statement
- Segment revenue and segment profit
- Geographic revenue
- Customer concentration
- Share count and per-share data
- Capital allocation
- Contractual commitments
- Company-specific operating KPIs
- Relevant customer, supplier and industry indicators

If 20 quarters are unavailable, use the maximum available period and explicitly state:

- Which quarters are missing
- Why they are unavailable
- Which metrics cannot be calculated reliably
- How the shorter history reduces confidence

### 4.2 Data-quality and normalization rules

The agent must:

- Use GAAP, IFRS or local statutory figures as the primary dataset
- Present adjusted or non-GAAP figures only as a secondary view
- Reconcile adjusted figures to reported figures
- Use restated historical figures when the company has restated results
- Record source, filing date, fiscal period, units and currency
- Preserve the company’s fiscal-quarter convention
- Identify 53-week years and unusual quarter lengths
- Adjust per-share data for stock splits
- Identify major accounting-policy changes
- Identify segment recasts and preserve both old and new definitions where required
- Separate continuing operations from discontinued operations
- Flag acquisitions, disposals, restructurings, impairments and litigation effects
- Separate organic growth from acquisition and currency effects where possible
- Distinguish capex from acquisitions, leases and capitalized development
- Use diluted weighted-average shares for quarterly per-share metrics
- Use ending diluted shares only where the formula specifically requires an ending share count
- Preserve raw reported data separately from normalized analytical data
- Document every formula and adjustment

The agent must never silently fill missing historical values. Missing values must remain identified as missing unless a derived estimate is explicitly labeled and justified.

### 4.3 Required analytical views

For each material metric, provide the appropriate combination of:

1. **Reported quarterly value** — detects immediate changes and inflections
2. **Rolling trailing-twelve-month value** — reduces seasonality and matches annualized return ratios
3. **Five-year historical distribution** — establishes normal, peak and trough conditions
4. **Peer or industry comparison** — tests whether changes are company-specific or industry-wide

For the five-year distribution, calculate where meaningful:

- Mean
- Median
- Minimum
- Maximum
- Standard deviation
- Coefficient of variation
- Current historical percentile
- Peak-to-trough change
- Number of positive and negative quarters
- One-year, three-year and five-year change
- Structural-break or change-point flags
- Seasonality by fiscal quarter

Statistical analysis may identify relationships, but correlation is not causation. Every material statistical relationship must be connected to a business mechanism before it is used in a forecast.

### 4.4 Core growth and scale metrics

For a normal non-financial operating company, collect and analyze:

- Revenue
- Revenue growth, year over year
- Organic revenue growth
- Gross profit
- Gross-profit growth
- Operating income
- Operating-income growth
- Net income
- Diluted EPS
- Operating cash flow
- Free cash flow
- Revenue per diluted share
- Operating income per diluted share
- Net income per diluted share
- Free cash flow per diluted share
- Diluted share count

The analysis must distinguish growth of the business from growth of the investor’s ownership claim.

### 4.5 Core margin metrics

Calculate, where economically meaningful:

```text
Gross margin = Gross profit ÷ Revenue
R&D margin = Research and development expense ÷ Revenue
Sales and marketing margin = Sales and marketing expense ÷ Revenue
G&A margin = General and administrative expense ÷ Revenue
SG&A margin = Selling, general and administrative expense ÷ Revenue
Operating margin = Operating income ÷ Revenue
EBITDA margin = EBITDA ÷ Revenue
NOPAT margin = NOPAT ÷ Revenue
Net income margin = Net income ÷ Revenue
Operating cash-flow margin = Operating cash flow ÷ Revenue
Free-cash-flow margin = Free cash flow ÷ Revenue
Capex margin = Capital expenditure ÷ Revenue
Stock-based compensation margin = Stock-based compensation ÷ Revenue
Cash-tax margin = Cash taxes paid ÷ Revenue
```

Where sales and marketing and G&A are disclosed separately, show them separately rather than relying only on combined SG&A.

The agent must explain whether margin changes arise from:

- Pricing
- Volume
- Product or customer mix
- Input costs
- Utilization
- Scale
- Temporary scarcity
- Competitive pressure
- Accounting changes
- Acquisitions
- Restructuring
- Underinvestment or overinvestment

### 4.6 Capital-efficiency and return metrics

Return metrics must use consistent definitions and average balance-sheet values.

Primary definitions:

```text
NOPAT = Operating income × (1 − Normalized cash tax rate)

Invested capital = Operating assets − Operating liabilities
```

A practical invested-capital bridge may include:

```text
Invested capital = Net working capital
                   + Net property, plant and equipment
                   + Capitalized operating intangibles
                   + Capitalized R&D where analytically appropriate
                   + Acquired operating assets
                   − Excess cash
                   − Non-operating investments
```

Calculate, where meaningful:

```text
ROIC = TTM NOPAT ÷ Average invested capital
ROCE = TTM EBIT ÷ Average capital employed
ROE = TTM net income attributable to common shareholders ÷ Average common equity
ROA = TTM net income ÷ Average total assets
FCF return on invested capital = TTM FCF ÷ Average invested capital
Invested-capital turnover = TTM revenue ÷ Average invested capital
Asset turnover = TTM revenue ÷ Average total assets
Sales-to-capital ratio = Change in revenue ÷ Incremental invested capital
Reinvestment rate = Net reinvestment ÷ NOPAT
```

Return ratios must primarily use rolling-TTM numerators and average balance-sheet denominators. A single quarter multiplied by four may be shown only as a secondary estimate and must be labeled as potentially distorted by seasonality.

### 4.7 Incremental economics

Calculate incremental economics over multiple horizons because one-year results can be noisy:

```text
Incremental operating margin = Change in operating income ÷ Change in revenue
Incremental FCF margin = Change in free cash flow ÷ Change in revenue
Incremental ROIC = Change in NOPAT ÷ Change in invested capital
```

Required intervals:

- One year
- Two years
- Three years
- Full five-year period

The agent must investigate negative or implausibly high incremental returns for:

- Working-capital reversals
- Acquisition accounting
- Asset disposals
- Capitalized costs
- Underinvestment
- Cyclical recovery from a depressed base
- Excess cash or non-operating assets

### 4.8 Cash-flow quality metrics

Calculate, where meaningful:

```text
CFO-to-net-income conversion = Operating cash flow ÷ Net income
FCF-to-net-income conversion = Free cash flow ÷ Net income
FCF-to-EBITDA conversion = Free cash flow ÷ EBITDA
Accrual ratio = (Net income − Operating cash flow) ÷ Average operating assets
```

Also analyze:

- Working-capital investment
- Cash interest coverage
- Maintenance-capex coverage
- Owner earnings
- Acquisition-adjusted free cash flow
- Free cash flow after recurring restructuring costs
- Free cash flow after the economic cost of stock-based compensation

Stock-based compensation must not be double counted. The agent must account for it through either dilution, replacement-cost analysis or an explicitly reconciled cash adjustment.

### 4.9 Working-capital and operating-efficiency metrics

Where relevant, calculate:

- Days sales outstanding
- Days inventory outstanding
- Days payable outstanding
- Cash-conversion cycle
- Inventory turns
- Fixed-asset turnover
- Inventory growth versus revenue growth
- Receivables growth versus revenue growth
- Payables growth versus cost of sales
- Deferred-revenue growth
- Contract liabilities
- Remaining performance obligations
- Backlog
- Book-to-bill
- Capacity utilization
- Revenue per employee
- Gross profit per employee

The agent must identify whether working-capital changes reflect growth, bargaining power, deterioration in demand quality or accounting timing.

### 4.10 Balance-sheet and capital-allocation metrics

Analyze, where relevant:

- Cash and liquid investments
- Net debt
- Net debt to EBITDA
- Debt to capital
- Interest coverage
- Fixed-charge coverage
- Current ratio
- Quick ratio
- Lease-adjusted leverage
- Goodwill and acquired intangibles as a percentage of assets
- Acquisition spending as a percentage of free cash flow
- Buybacks as a percentage of free cash flow
- Dividends as a percentage of free cash flow
- Net share-count change
- Stock-based compensation as a percentage of revenue and FCF
- Buyback offset ratio
- Tangible book value per share
- Retained earnings growth
- Contractual commitments and purchase obligations

Capital allocation must be analyzed on a per-share basis, not only in aggregate.

### 4.11 Required metric diagnosis

For every core metric, the agent must explain:

| Dimension | Required question |
|---|---|
| Level | What is the current quarter and TTM value? |
| History | How does it compare with the five-year median, peak and trough? |
| Trend | Is it improving, stable or deteriorating? |
| Stability | How volatile is it and how often does it reverse? |
| Inflection | When did the current trend begin? |
| Cause | Which business-process node caused the movement? |
| Control | Was the change controlled by management or imposed externally? |
| Sustainability | Is it structural, cyclical, temporary or accounting-driven? |
| Moat implication | Does the metric support or contradict a claimed moat? |
| Forecast implication | Which model input must change because of the evidence? |

### 4.12 Required decompositions

Where possible, decompose:

```text
ROIC = NOPAT margin × Invested-capital turnover
ROE = Net margin × Asset turnover × Financial leverage
Revenue growth = Volume + Price + Mix + Currency + Acquisitions − Cannibalization
FCF margin = Operating margin − Cash taxes − Working capital − Net capital investment
Per-share growth = Business growth + Buyback effect − Dilution
```

The agent must avoid attributing a high ROE to business quality when it is primarily created by financial leverage or a small equity base.

### 4.13 Required 20-quarter outputs

The final research package must include, where relevant:

1. Revenue and year-over-year revenue growth
2. Gross profit and gross margin
3. R&D, sales and marketing, G&A and SG&A margins
4. Operating, net income, CFO and FCF margins
5. Capex and reinvestment margins
6. ROIC, ROCE, ROE and ROA
7. Incremental operating margin and incremental ROIC
8. Asset turnover and invested-capital turnover
9. CFO and FCF conversion
10. Working-capital metrics
11. Diluted shares and per-share value creation
12. Debt, liquidity and contractual commitments
13. Company-specific operating KPIs

Each chart or table should include the appropriate quarterly series, rolling-TTM series, five-year median and relevant peer or industry benchmark.

### 4.14 Business-model-specific substitution rule

The universal metric set must not be applied blindly.

For banks, insurers, REITs, commodity producers, early-stage companies and other specialized models, replace economically inappropriate metrics with sector-relevant measures while preserving the same analytical objectives: profitability, capital efficiency, cash or distributable earnings, risk, growth quality and per-share value creation.

Examples include:

- **Banks and lenders:** net interest margin, efficiency ratio, deposit beta, charge-offs, non-performing loans, CET1, tangible-book-value growth and ROTCE
- **Insurers:** combined ratio, loss ratio, expense ratio, reserve development, book value and return on equity
- **REITs:** same-property NOI, occupancy, leasing spreads, AFFO, leverage, interest coverage and NAV
- **SaaS:** ARR, gross retention, net retention, CAC payback, sales efficiency, RPO and deferred revenue
- **Semiconductors:** units, ASP, utilization, inventory days, channel inventory, customer concentration, product-transition charges and performance per watt
- **Commodity producers:** production, realized price, cash cost, sustaining capital, reserve life and FCF at spot and mid-cycle prices

The agent must state why any standard metric was omitted or replaced.

## 5. Mandatory Moat Analysis and Attack

A moat is a durable mechanism that protects customer preference, market share, pricing, margins, cash flows or reinvestment returns from competition and substitution.

High growth, high margins, market leadership or a popular brand are not sufficient proof of a moat. They may reflect temporary scarcity, a favorable cycle, regulatory protection, competitor underinvestment or accounting treatment.

The agent must identify the mechanism, test the evidence and attempt to break the moat before using it in a forecast or terminal valuation.

### 7.1 Separate moat source from moat evidence

For every claimed moat, distinguish:

| Layer | Required analysis |
|---|---|
| Moat source | The structural advantage claimed |
| Customer consequence | How it changes customer behavior |
| Competitor consequence | Why rivals cannot easily match the outcome |
| Company value capture | How the company converts the advantage into economics |
| Financial evidence | Historical metrics supporting the claim |
| Operating evidence | Retention, share, usage, pricing or productivity evidence |
| Attack vector | How the advantage could be weakened |
| Maintenance requirement | Capital, R&D, service, distribution or ecosystem spending required |
| Failure indicator | Evidence the moat is decaying |

### 7.2 Moat categories

Test, where relevant:

- Switching costs
- Direct network effects
- Indirect or ecosystem network effects
- Cost advantage
- Economies of scale
- Brand and trust
- Intellectual property
- Proprietary technology
- Distribution advantage
- Data advantage
- Regulatory licenses or standards position
- Process or operational advantage
- Scarce physical assets
- Customer workflow integration
- Culture and talent system
- Capital or balance-sheet advantage
- Installed-base advantage
- Learning-curve advantage

Do not count the same underlying mechanism as multiple separate moats.

### 7.3 Mandatory moat proof test

A moat cannot be classified as durable unless the agent can answer:

1. What profit pool is being protected?
2. Which customers are affected?
3. What behavior demonstrates the advantage?
4. Why can competitors not reproduce it quickly?
5. What does a customer lose by switching?
6. Can customers multi-source, vertically integrate or adopt an open standard?
7. Does the advantage protect revenue, margin, retention, capital efficiency or all four?
8. How much annual reinvestment is required to maintain it?
9. Is the advantage strengthening, stable or decaying?
10. Which 20-quarter metrics support or contradict the claim?

If the evidence is insufficient, classify the moat as **unproven**, regardless of management claims or market reputation.

### 7.4 Mandatory moat attack matrix

For every claimed moat, complete:

| Field | Required analysis |
|---|---|
| Claimed moat | Exact advantage being tested |
| Protected economics | Revenue, share, pricing, margin, retention or ROIC protected |
| Evidence | Customer, operating and financial proof |
| Primary attacker | Competitor, customer, supplier, regulator, substitute or open-source system |
| Attack mechanism | How the economics could be disrupted |
| Capital required to attack | Estimated financial and organizational requirement |
| Time required | Time to replicate, bypass or displace |
| First vulnerable customer | Customer segment most likely to leave |
| Customer switching path | Practical steps required to switch or multi-source |
| Incumbent defense | Product, price, bundling, legal, acquisition or ecosystem response |
| Leading indicators | Early evidence the attack is succeeding |
| Financial translation | Impact on volume, price, margin, reinvestment and ROIC |
| Residual moat | Advantage remaining after a partial attack |
| Confidence | High, moderate or low |

### 7.5 Required red-team attacks

The agent must test at least the following attacks where relevant:

- A well-capitalized direct competitor reduces price by 20–30%
- The largest customers build or source an internal alternative
- Customers adopt multi-vendor or multi-cloud architectures
- An open standard removes interoperability lock-in
- A substitute solves the same problem differently
- Regulation forces access, portability, pricing changes or structural separation
- A supplier captures more of the value chain
- Distribution shifts to a channel the company does not control
- Technology lowers the value of the installed base
- A new entrant uses a different business model and accepts lower margins
- The company underinvests in the moat to protect short-term margins
- The company overinvests and destroys incremental returns

For each attack, state whether the moat survives fully, partially or not at all.

### 7.6 Moat scoring dimensions

Score each moat from 0 to 5 on:

- Current strength
- Breadth across revenue
- Depth of customer dependence
- Replication difficulty
- Substitution resistance
- Pricing power
- Retention support
- Cost or productivity advantage
- Value capture by the company
- Maintenance burden
- Attack resistance
- Expected durability
- Decay velocity

The score is a structured judgment, not a mechanical valuation input.

Classify the moat as:

- **Unproven**
- **Weak**
- **Moderate**
- **Strong**
- **Exceptional**

Provide a separate confidence classification.

### 7.7 Historical financial evidence for the moat

Every moat claim must be tested against the 20-quarter record.

Examples:

| Claimed moat | Supporting evidence | Contradicting evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Pricing power | Stable or rising gross margin despite cost inflation | Volume loss or promotional spending after price increases |
| Switching costs | High retention and growing spend per customer | Multi-sourcing, falling retention or lower renewal rates |
| Scale advantage | Lower unit costs and improving capital turns | SG&A, capex or working capital rising as fast as revenue |
| Network effects | Better retention and activity as the network grows | Falling engagement per participant or successful multi-homing |
| Brand | Premium pricing and stable market share | Rising acquisition or promotional expense required to defend share |
| Ecosystem lock-in | Growing attachment, usage and retention | Interoperable alternatives gaining adoption |
| Cost advantage | Superior margins through multiple cycles | Advantage disappears when scarcity or cycle conditions normalize |

The agent must distinguish structural moat evidence from cyclical or temporary economics.

### 5.8 Moat-to-model translation

The moat conclusion must explicitly affect:

- Market-share path
- Customer retention
- New-customer win rate
- Pricing and discount assumptions
- Gross margin
- Sales and marketing intensity
- R&D and moat-maintenance spending
- Capital intensity
- Incremental ROIC
- Forecast fade period
- Terminal margin
- Terminal growth
- Required return or uncertainty range

A strong moat does not automatically justify a high valuation. It only changes the durability and confidence of the forecasted economics.

### 5.9 Moat decay scenarios

Construct at least three moat states:

| State | Required description |
|---|---|
| Strengthening | Evidence that customer dependence, economics or replication difficulty is increasing |
| Stable | Advantage persists but requires continuing reinvestment |
| Decaying | Attackers reduce switching costs, pricing power, share or returns |

The operating bear, base and bull cases must state which moat state is assumed and why.

### 5.10 No-moat counterfactual

The agent must estimate what the business would look like without its claimed moat.

At minimum, estimate the likely effect on:

- Market share
- Price
- Gross margin
- Operating margin
- Customer acquisition cost
- Retention
- Capital intensity
- ROIC
- Valuation multiple or terminal economics

This counterfactual tests how much of the current valuation depends on the moat remaining intact.

## 6. Research Process

## Stage 1: Define the Asset

State:

- Asset type
- Claim held by the investor
- Source of economic value
- Relevant currency
- Investment horizon
- Primary valuation method
- Major non-financial risks
- Unit of analysis
- Major segments requiring separate models

Examples of claims:

- Equity: residual claim on future cash flows
- Bond: contractual cash flows plus recovery value
- Commodity: exposure to scarcity and marginal production economics
- Cryptoasset: claim on network utility, monetary premium, fees or collateral demand
- Real estate: property-level cash flows and residual land value
- Fund or holding company: look-through value of underlying assets

---

## Stage 2: Establish the Evidence Base

Use the following source hierarchy:

1. Regulatory filings and audited reports
2. Official company, government or protocol data
3. Industry bodies and primary datasets
4. Customer and supplier disclosures
5. Reputable independent research
6. Management commentary
7. Analyst estimates
8. Media and secondary commentary
9. Social media and anecdotal evidence

Management claims must be independently tested where possible.

Record for each material source:

- Date
- Reporting period
- Definition used
- Currency and units
- Whether it is historical, estimated or promotional
- Whether it refers to orders, shipments, deployments, usage, revenue or cash
- Whether it is company-wide, segment-specific or market-wide

Do not combine statistics using inconsistent definitions.

### Evidence table

| Claim | Source | Period | Definition | Classification | Reliability | Model use |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

---

## Stage 3: Build the 20-Quarter Historical Record

Collect, validate and normalize the latest 20 completed fiscal quarters before forecasting.

Required actions:

1. Build raw reported income-statement, balance-sheet and cash-flow datasets.
2. Collect segment and company-specific KPI history.
3. Record every source, definition, unit, fiscal period and restatement.
4. Preserve both reported and normalized datasets.
5. Reconcile non-GAAP measures to statutory results.
6. Identify acquisitions, divestitures, accounting changes and one-off items.
7. Calculate quarterly and rolling-TTM series using consistent definitions.
8. Document missing information and confidence effects.

No operating forecast may be finalized until the historical record is complete or the missing evidence is explicitly disclosed.

---

## Stage 4: Diagnose Financial and KPI History

Analyze the 20-quarter history across:

- Growth and scale
- Margin structure
- Operating leverage
- Cash conversion
- Working capital
- Capital intensity
- ROIC, ROCE, ROE and ROA
- Incremental returns
- Balance-sheet risk
- Capital allocation
- Dilution
- Per-share value creation
- Company-specific operating KPIs

For every material metric change, identify:

1. The quarter in which the change began
2. The business-process node responsible
3. Whether the cause was structural, cyclical, temporary or accounting-driven
4. Whether management controlled the outcome
5. Whether the movement supports or contradicts a claimed moat
6. Which forecast assumption must change

The agent must not proceed with a forecast that contradicts the historical record without explaining the process change that makes the future different.

---

## Stage 5: Decompose the Business and Value Chain

Map the complete value chain:

```text
Upstream inputs
→ suppliers
→ company operations
→ distribution or deployment
→ direct customer
→ end user
→ economic outcome
```

For every major segment, identify:

- Product or service
- Customer
- End user
- Revenue model
- Contract structure
- Sales channel
- Suppliers
- Complements
- Competitors
- Substitutes
- Regulatory dependencies
- Key physical or organizational constraints

Do not aggregate segments with materially different economics.

---

## Stage 6: Map the Customer Decision Process

For each customer group, explain:

1. What problem initiates demand?
2. Who identifies the need?
3. Who approves the budget?
4. What alternatives are considered?
5. What economic return is required?
6. What delays deployment?
7. What determines vendor selection?
8. What determines purchase size?
9. What causes renewal, expansion or replacement?
10. What causes cancellation, churn or substitution?

A company-level growth forecast is incomplete without customer-level decision analysis.

---

## Stage 7: Map Supply, Operations and Delivery

Determine how the company fulfills demand.

Analyze:

- Production or service capacity
- Supplier concentration
- Procurement lead times
- Inventory policy
- Manufacturing yield or labor productivity
- Logistics
- Installation
- Customer acceptance
- Service capacity
- Quality control
- Warranty exposure
- Regulatory approval
- Revenue-recognition conditions

Identify the limiting operational bottleneck for each forecast year.

---

## Stage 8: Identify the Economic Engine

Identify no more than five principal value drivers per major segment.

For each driver, explain:

- Current level
- Historical trend
- Causal relationship to value
- Process node through which it operates
- Industry constraint
- Leading indicators
- Failure conditions
- Financial line affected
- Time lag

Typical drivers include:

- Customers
- Adoption
- Utilization
- Volume
- Market penetration
- Market share
- Price or take rate
- Retention
- Capacity
- Unit costs
- Capital intensity
- Credit losses
- Reinvestment returns
- Supply growth
- Network activity
- Regulatory access
- Replacement cycles
- Product mix
- Software or service attachment

Separate:

- **Demand drivers**
- **Customer-economic drivers**
- **Conversion and deployment drivers**
- **Growth drivers**
- **Pricing drivers**
- **Margin drivers**
- **Reinvestment requirements**
- **Balance-sheet risks**
- **Valuation drivers**

---

## Stage 9: Conduct Moat Analysis and Attack

Identify each claimed moat, test it against customer behavior and the 20-quarter historical record, then attempt to break it.

Required actions:

1. Separate the moat source from the financial outcome.
2. Identify the customer behavior protected by the moat.
3. Identify the profit pool and economic variable protected.
4. Test switching, substitution, vertical integration, multi-sourcing and open-standard attacks.
5. Estimate the capital and time required to attack the moat.
6. Identify the first vulnerable customer segment.
7. Evaluate the company’s likely defensive response.
8. Link moat strength to pricing, retention, share, margins, reinvestment and ROIC.
9. Construct strengthening, stable and decaying moat states.
10. Build a no-moat counterfactual.

No market-share, pricing, margin or terminal-value assumption may rely on a moat that has not passed this stage.

---

## Stage 10: Build the Driver Tree

Every material financial line must be decomposed into operational drivers.

### Generic operating company

```text
Revenue = Customers × Purchase frequency × Units per purchase × Price × Retention
```

### Industrial or hardware company

```text
Revenue = Addressable projects × Funding rate × Company win rate × Units per project × Price per unit
```

Then adjust for:

```text
Deployment capacity
Delivery timing
Product mix
Replacement demand
Cancellations
Channel inventory
```

### Subscription business

```text
Ending customers = Beginning customers + New customers − Churned customers
Revenue = Average customers × Seats × Usage × Price
```

### Marketplace

```text
Revenue = Buyers × Transactions per buyer × Gross transaction value × Take rate
```

### Bank or lender

```text
Net income = Earning assets × Net interest margin
             + Fee income
             − Operating costs
             − Credit losses
             − Taxes
```

### Commodity producer

```text
Free cash flow = Production × Realized price
                 − Cash costs
                 − Royalties and taxes
                 − Sustaining capital
                 − Growth capital
```

### Cryptoasset

```text
Economic value = Network usage
                 + Fee capture
                 + Security demand
                 + Collateral demand
                 + Monetary premium
                 − Token dilution
                 − Required incentives
```

Do not force a DCF onto an asset without measurable distributable cash flows.

## Stage 12: Quantify Process States

Translate each process outcome into measurable model inputs.

Examples:

| Process outcome | Quantitative model input |
|---|---|
| Faster customer adoption | Penetration rate or new-customer count |
| Deployment bottleneck | Maximum units installed per year |

## Stage 13: Build the Operating Model

Construct the smallest model capable of explaining the asset, while retaining enough process detail to make scenarios meaningful.

### Required structure

1. Market or industry build
2. Customer and end-demand build
3. Customer budget and decision conversion
4. Asset-level win rate or adoption
5. Operational capacity and delivery
6. Volume or usage
7. Pricing and mix
8. Revenue or economic output
9. Operating costs
10. Reinvestment
11. Working capital
12. Cash flow or economic earnings
13. Balance-sheet effects
14. Per-share or per-unit value

### Required revenue bridge

For equities:

```text
Revenue growth = Volume growth + Pricing + Mix + Acquisitions − Cannibalization
```

But each component must itself be driver-based.

Example:

```text
Volume growth = Market demand growth
                × Budget conversion
                × Company win rate
                × Deployment rate
                × Delivery rate
```

### Required margin bridge

```text
Change in operating margin = Price effect
                             + Product-mix effect
                             + Input-cost effect
                             + Utilization effect
                             + Scale effect
                             + Competitive effect
                             + Reinvestment effect
```

### Required cash-flow bridge

```text
Change in free cash flow = Change in after-tax operating profit
                           − Incremental working capital
                           − Incremental capital expenditure
                           − Other economic reinvestment
```

## Stage 14: Reconcile Top-Down and Bottom-Up Forecasts

The agent must build both:

### Top-down view

```text
Industry demand × Addressable share × Company capture
```

### Bottom-up view

```text
Customers × Units or usage × Price × Delivery timing
```

Then reconcile differences.

The agent must show:

- Implied industry size
- Implied customer spending
- Implied market share
- Implied production or deployment capacity
- Implied customer count or installed base
- Implied capital and infrastructure requirements

If the top-down and bottom-up forecasts do not converge within a reasonable range, explain why and reduce confidence.

---

## Stage 15: Test Reinvestment Economics

Growth is only valuable when incremental returns exceed the required return.

Estimate:

```text
Incremental ROIC = Change in after-tax operating profit ÷ Incremental invested capital
```

Consider:

- Capital expenditure
- Working capital
- Acquisitions
- Credit capital
- Customer incentives
- Research and development
- Token incentives
- Inventory
- Leases
- Infrastructure
- Supplier prepayments
- Capacity reservations
- Guarantees
- Customer financing
- Strategic investments

Ask:

- How much capital is required before revenue appears?
- Who provides the capital: the company, customer, supplier or government?
- Is the return measurable?
- Does return improve with scale?
- Does growth become less efficient over time?
- Are reported margins hiding capital requirements elsewhere?
- Does the company externalize capital requirements to suppliers or customers?
- What happens if demand slows after commitments are made?

---

## Stage 16: Analyze Management and Capital Allocation

Determine whether management can convert the opportunity into per-share value.

Analyze:

- R&D allocation
- Capacity investment
- Acquisitions
- Buybacks
- Dividends
- Debt issuance
- Equity issuance
- Stock-based compensation
- Strategic investments
- Customer or supplier financing
- Incentive structure

The agent must distinguish:

```text
Business growth
from
Per-share value growth
```

Growth that requires excessive dilution, acquisitions or uneconomic commitments may not create shareholder value.

---

## Stage 17: Construct Independent Scenarios

Keep operating performance separate from valuation assumptions.

### Operating cases

- **Bear:** important business processes weaken or fail
- **Base:** reasonable process outcomes and normal execution
- **Bull:** strong process outcomes without assuming perfection

### Valuation cases

- **Conservative:** high required return and restrained terminal assumptions
- **Base:** normal required return
- **Aggressive:** lower required return or stronger terminal economics

Produce a 3 × 3 operating-versus-valuation matrix.

Do not automatically combine:

- Bear operations with the highest discount rate
- Bull operations with the lowest discount rate

The scenarios should be independently selectable.

### Required scenario narrative

For each operating case, explain:

1. End-market condition
2. Customer economics
3. Customer spending behavior
4. Competitive response
5. Company execution
6. Capacity and deployment
7. Pricing and mix
8. Margin formation
9. Reinvestment and working capital
10. Resulting revenue, cash flow and balance-sheet outcome

Only after this narrative is complete may the agent assign numerical growth rates.

---

## Stage 18: Select the Correct Valuation Methods

Use methods appropriate to the asset.

| Asset | Primary methods |
|---|---|
| Operating company | DCF, reverse DCF, comparable multiples |
| Holding company | SOTP, NAV discount, capital-allocation analysis |
| Bank or lender | Excess-return model, P/B versus ROE, dividend discount |
| Commodity producer | Mid-cycle NAV, commodity sensitivity, replacement cost |
| Commodity | Cost curve, inventory balance, incentive price |
| Bond | Yield, spread, default probability, recovery value |
| Real estate | Property-level cash flow, cap rate, NAV |
| Cryptoasset | Network value, fee economics, monetary premium, dilution |
| Early-stage asset | Scenario value, probability-weighted outcomes, runway analysis |

Use at least two valuation methods where possible.

A precise DCF output must be presented as a range, not a point estimate.

Valuation cannot repair an incomplete operating model.

---

## Stage 19: Perform a Reverse Valuation

Determine what the current price implies.

Solve for one or more of:

- Revenue growth
- Customer growth
- Usage
- Market penetration
- Market share
- Win rate
- Pricing
- Operating margin
- Commodity price
- Loan-loss rate
- Terminal growth
- Network usage
- Required return

Then translate the implied financial assumption back into business-process requirements.

Do not stop at:

> The market implies 20% revenue growth.

Instead state:

> The current price requires approximately 20% revenue growth, which would require the addressable market to reach X, customer budgets to grow by Y, the company to retain Z share, deployment capacity to expand by A, pricing to decline by no more than B, and margins to remain near C.

Then classify the implied outcome as:

- Easy
- Reasonable
- Demanding
- Historically unprecedented
- Operationally inconsistent

---

## Stage 20: Identify Thesis Breakers

List the three to seven events that would invalidate the analysis.

Examples:

- Market-share loss
- Customer ROI deteriorates
- Budget growth stops
- Deployment capacity falls short
- Unit economics deteriorate
- Incremental ROIC falls below cost of capital
- Credit losses exceed expected yield
- Regulation impairs monetization
- Supply growth removes scarcity
- Technology cannibalizes rather than expands value
- Management allocates capital poorly
- Dilution exceeds operating-value growth
- Replacement cycles extend
- Competitors force price reductions
- Backlog fails to convert into cash

Each thesis breaker must be linked to:

- A process node
- A measurable indicator
- A threshold
- A financial impact
- A required model change

---

## Stage 21: Estimate Expected Return

Do not use fair-value upside alone.

```text
Expected return = Fundamental value growth
                  + Cash yield
                  + Valuation change
                  − Dilution
```

Provide:

- Bear return
- Base return
- Bull return
- Probability-weighted expected return
- Maximum plausible permanent loss
- Expected return without multiple expansion
- Expected return if the current discount or premium remains unchanged
- Expected return if the business performs well but valuation normalizes
- Expected return if revenue grows but margins decline

Full convergence to fair value must not automatically be treated as the base case.

## 9. Agent Behaviour Rules

The agent must:

- Test every moat claim against customer behavior and historical economics
- Complete a moat attack matrix and no-moat counterfactual
- Link moat conclusions to share, pricing, margins, retention, reinvestment, ROIC and terminal assumptions

- Build a process map before assigning growth rates

- Identify the narrowest operational bottleneck
- Model customer economics

- Use ranges when evidence is weak

- Reconcile top-down and bottom-up forecasts

- Test the thesis before supporting it

- Translate reverse-valuation outputs into operational requirements

## 10. Research Completeness Gates

The analysis is incomplete unless all gates are passed.

### Gate 1: Evidence integrity

Sources, definitions, periods, currencies, restatements and adjustments are documented.

### Gate 2: Twenty-quarter record

The latest 20 completed fiscal quarters are collected, normalized and reconciled, or missing history is explicitly disclosed.

### Gate 3: Historical diagnosis

Core financial and operating KPIs are analyzed at quarterly and rolling-TTM levels, with causes and forecast implications identified.

### Gate 4: Business understanding

The agent can explain the product, customer, supplier, competitor and end-user relationships without relying on management slogans.

### Gate 5: Customer economics

The agent can explain why customers buy, how the purchase is approved and what would cause spending to slow.

### Gate 6: Operational conversion

The agent can map demand to delivery, revenue recognition and cash collection.

### Gate 7: Constraint analysis

The agent has identified the binding bottlenecks and reconciled the forecast to them.

### Gate 8: Moat proof

Each claimed moat is supported by customer behavior, operating evidence and 20-quarter financial evidence.

### Gate 9: Moat attack

The agent has tested competitor, customer, substitute, regulatory and open-standard attacks and estimated residual moat strength.

### Gate 10: Competitive response

The agent has identified who loses economics if the company succeeds and how they may react.

### Gate 11: Driver-based model

Every major forecast line is generated from operational drivers rather than an unsupported growth rate.

### Gate 12: Scenario coherence

Bear, base and bull cases reflect internally consistent process and moat outcomes.

### Gate 13: Reinvestment

The capital, working-capital and moat-maintenance requirements of growth are modeled.

### Gate 14: Reverse expectations

The current price is translated into required operating, moat and financial outcomes.

### Gate 15: Monitoring

Every material assumption has a leading indicator and failure threshold.

If any gate fails, the agent must state that the research is incomplete and reduce confidence.

## 11. Research Standard

Before reaching a conclusion, the agent must be able to answer:

1. What is the asset’s economic engine?
2. Is the latest 20-quarter historical record complete and internally consistent?
3. Which financial and KPI trends are structural, cyclical, temporary or accounting-driven?
4. How does the business actually operate from end demand to collected cash?
5. Who makes the critical purchase or allocation decision?
6. What economic return does that decision maker receive?
7. What are the three most important process nodes?
8. What constrains those processes?
9. What is the narrowest bottleneck?
10. What are the company’s claimed moats?
11. What customer behavior and historical financial evidence prove each moat?
12. How can competitors, customers, suppliers, regulators or substitutes attack each moat?
13. What remains if the moat is partially or fully broken?
14. What does the current price already assume?
15. What customer budgets and market share are implied?
16. What operational capacity is required?
17. How much reinvestment and moat-maintenance spending is required?
18. What is the incremental return on that reinvestment?
19. Are ROIC, ROE, ROA and ROCE improving for sound economic reasons?
20. Is free cash flow compounding faster than diluted shares?
21. How will competitors and customers respond?
22. What would invalidate the thesis?
23. What happens without valuation expansion?
24. What happens under moat decay?
25. Where could double counting exist?
26. Which assumptions are facts, estimates or speculation?
27. Which leading indicators would reveal a change before reported earnings?
28. Why does each scenario produce its stated financial result?
29. What would have to be true for the forecast to be wrong?
30. Does the historical record support the terminal economics used in valuation?

If these questions cannot be answered, the research is incomplete.

## 12. Decision Standard

Conclude with one classification:

- **Attractive**
- **Watchlist**
- **Fully valued**
- **Speculative**
- **Avoid**

The conclusion must be supported by:

- Expected return
- Downside
- Confidence
- Thesis durability
- Business-process durability
- Five-year historical evidence
- Moat strength and durability
- Customer economics
- Competitive protection
- Reinvestment returns
- Valuation protection

A compelling story without operational and historical evidence is not sufficient.

A claimed moat that has not survived an adversarial attack is not sufficient.

A high growth rate without a causal business-process build is not sufficient.

A low valuation without a durable economic engine is not sufficient.

The preferred investment has:

```text
Durable economics
+ Strong customer value
+ Defensible business processes
+ Proven and attack-resistant moat
+ High incremental returns
+ Reasonable market expectations
+ Adequate margin of safety
```

## 13. Mandatory Agent Enforcement Block

The following instructions must be treated as binding whenever this framework is used:

> Do not assign revenue, margin or cash-flow growth rates before completing the business-process map, the 20-quarter historical record and the moat attack. For every major forecast, explain the customer decision, operational process, constraint, competitive response, moat state, time lag and financial translation that creates the number. Bear, base and bull cases must be built from different process and moat outcomes, not arbitrary percentage changes. Every moat claim must be supported by customer behavior, operating evidence and historical financial evidence, then tested against competitor, customer, supplier, substitute, regulatory and open-standard attacks. Return metrics must use consistent definitions and average balance-sheet values. If the evidence is insufficient, use a range, reduce confidence and state what information is missing. The analysis is not complete until the current valuation has been translated back into the real-world customer spending, market share, capacity, pricing, margins, moat durability and reinvestment required to justify it.

### Hard enforcement rules

> **No moat without evidence:** A competitive advantage cannot be classified as a moat unless it is supported by customer behavior, operating evidence and financial outcomes.

> **No historical table without interpretation:** Presenting 20 quarters of data is insufficient. The agent must explain the causes, sustainability and forecast implications of material movements.

> **No return ratio without denominator discipline:** ROIC, ROE, ROA and ROCE must use consistent definitions and average balance-sheet values.

> **No TTM masking:** TTM figures must not be used to conceal recent quarterly deterioration.

> **No quarterly overreaction:** A single quarterly change must not be classified as structural without supporting process evidence.

> **No universal metric misuse:** Metrics that do not fit the company’s economic model must be replaced with appropriate sector-specific measures.

> **No moat permanence assumption:** Every moat must have a maintenance requirement, attack path, residual-moat assessment and decay scenario.

> **No forecast disconnected from history:** Forecast margins, capital efficiency and per-share economics must be reconciled with the 20-quarter record.
